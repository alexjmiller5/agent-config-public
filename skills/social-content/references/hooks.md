# Hooks and truthful curiosity

A hook tells the right reader what is at stake and gives them a reason to
continue. The title makes a promise; the opening starts paying it off.
Curiosity works best when the subject is clear and the unresolved part is
worth learning. Do not hide the entire subject behind "this" or "one thing."

## Build the angle before the sentence

Write down the most interesting supported fact and the question it creates.
Generate different reasons to care, not five synonyms for the same pitch.
Choose from this editorial toolbox, not a mandatory formula:

| Angle | Useful construction | Evidence needed |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Specific friction | Name the annoying step and the practical alternative | The alternative actually handles that step |
| Unexpected constraint | Pair an outcome with the condition that makes it interesting | Both the outcome and constraint are documented |
| Demonstration | Start with the observable result, then invite inspection of the method | A real result or clearly labeled staged demo |
| Contrast | Put an expectation beside an observed difference | A real comparison; don't invent what everyone believes |
| Bounded question | Ask something the content can actually help answer | An answer, method, or genuine discussion in the body |
| Consequence | Name what the problem can affect and how to inspect it | Support for the consequence; distinguish risk from an incident |
| Story turn | Introduce the decision or discovery that changed the author's approach | The author's actual experience |

Numbers help when their meaning matters. A tiny beta's signup count is not
automatically a reader benefit. Use that count for a beta retrospective;
lead with the problem or demonstrated capability for a practical tool post.

## Keep the promise inside the evidence

Internally pair each candidate with its supporting fact and required payoff.
Check both direct statements and insinuations. Turning a false assertion
into a question does not fix it. A limitation buried in paragraph six does
not repair a misleading headline.

- "Compares export row counts" cannot become "verifies your data is intact."
- "Seven beta participants used it last week" cannot become "users keep
  coming back" without cohort and time-series evidence.
- A demo showing a failed restore followed by another backup cannot end
  with "problem solved" unless the successful restore is also established.
- Avoid invented scarcity, consensus, secret knowledge, expertise,
  testimonials, guarantees, and fabricated personal stakes.

## Worked example

Facts: a free script compares row counts before and after CSV export. It
flags unequal counts; it does not compare field values. No measured savings.

- Clear: "A free script to compare row counts before and after CSV export"
- More curiosity: "The export finished. Did the row counts match?"
- More practical: "Check CSV row-count mismatches before the next import"
- Reject: "Your exports are silently corrupting data. This script fixes it."

The body should explain the comparison immediately and distinguish equal
counts from full integrity. None of the valid titles invents an incident.

## Pick and revise

Rank by audience relevance, concrete meaning, reason to continue, proof,
and destination fit. Use editorial judgment, not invented virality scores.
If the facts are thin, find a narrower interesting angle or ask for a real
example; do not replace missing substance with urgency. Keep the strongest
version that still feels fair after reading the entire post.
