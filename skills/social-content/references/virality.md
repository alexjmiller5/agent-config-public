# Understanding and learning from virality

Use "viral" as a description of spread, not a promised result. Attention,
continued consumption, sharing, and useful outcomes are separate decisions.
A title can earn a click while the body loses the reader's trust.

## Reasons to care and share

Use these as questions to generate angles, not claims about an algorithm:

- **Practical value:** Can someone solve a recognizable problem or avoid
  repeating work? What could they save or send to a colleague?
- **Social meaning:** Would sharing help someone explain an experience,
  support another person, or express a considered point of view?
- **Emotion:** Is there a real source of surprise, delight, relief, awe,
  humor, or tension? Do not manufacture outrage when the evidence is ordinary.
- **Recall:** What recurring situation would make this idea useful again?
- **Story:** Is there a concrete choice, obstacle, demonstration, or outcome
  people can retell? Keep the useful point attached to the story.

Research on emotional arousal and sharing motivates some of these lenses.
It does not establish a platform-independent recipe or a probability that
a particular draft will spread. See [sources](sources.md).

## Research current demand and competitors

Before original ideation, establish the niche, destination, audience, and
goal from the brief. Then inspect accessible originals, not just a tool's
trend summary or search snippets:

1. Search recent discussions about the problem and adjacent topics. Look
   for recurring questions, new developments, and what people want explained.
2. Inspect relevant competitors and peer creators serving the same audience.
   Include community contributors when there are no direct competitors.
   Read their successful recent posts AND ordinary or weak comparisons.
3. Read comments and the full content. Identify the promise, actual payoff,
   evidence, objections, unanswered questions, and requests for follow-ups.
4. Record the source URL or supplied identifier, publication and observation
   dates when known, community, topic, format, visible metrics, and incentives.
   Do not invent missing data or describe old examples as currently trending.
5. Turn the findings into a few options: observed demand + the author's own
   contribution + a truthful hook. Attach the relevant sources and a short
   reason to each option. Adapt useful patterns without copying another
   creator's wording, experiences, or unsupported claims.

Start with a manageable sample and expand only when needed. Choose a recent
window appropriate to the niche and its pace; record that window. Distinguish
an emerging discussion from repeated demand and an older enduring pattern.
One new post is a lead to investigate, not proof of a trend. Check whether
similar posts already answer the question; a new contribution needs a reason
to exist. Use vendor trend or outlier scores to find examples, not as proof
that an angle works. If supplied research is sufficient, use it; if access
is missing, disclose the gap and offer provisional angles.

## Compare performance without overclaiming

Compare like with like: similar audiences, formats, topics, and observation
windows. A six-month giveaway and a thirty-minute tutorial are not useful
controls for a week-old tutorial. Raw totals, votes per hour, and follower
ratios do not remove exposure, selection, or survivorship bias. Without
impressions, do not invent an impression-based engagement rate.

For a useful example, extract:

1. What the audience already cared about.
2. The title's concrete promise and unanswered question.
3. How the opening and body delivered it.
4. Evidence and limitations of the observed performance.
5. A reusable structural choice, filled with the new author's own facts.

Phrase explanations as hypotheses: "This specific failure may make the
tutorial relevant" is supported more readily than "This hook caused 2x
reach." A vendor's score or AI explanation is not causal evidence.

## Learn from the next post

Pick the metric that serves the goal: useful replies for discussion,
completion for a video, saves for a reference, qualified clicks for a
resource, or actual adoption for a product. Use only metrics the platform
exposes. Examine hostile or confused comments before calling comment volume
a success. Avoid claiming an A/B test from differently timed organic posts.

Record what changed, when results were observed, what happened, and what
to try next. Maintain audience examples and performance records as user
data outside the skill's source repository. Refresh community rules and
platform facts when using them; do not fossilize posting-time folklore.
