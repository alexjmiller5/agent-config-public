# Video scripts and retention

Choose the viewer's question and the demonstrated payoff before writing
voiceover. The opening should make the clip worth watching quickly; a
three-second opening is a useful editing target for a short clip, not an
algorithmic law. Match length and production style to the actual assignment.

## Pick the narrative

| Material | Structure |
| --- | --- |
| Tutorial or demo | Visible result/problem, necessary context, reproducible steps, verification |
| Personal experience | Concrete moment, choice, consequence, useful reflection |
| Explanation | Specific question, mechanism, example, answer with limits |
| Comparison | Shared task and criteria, comparable demonstrations, tradeoff |
| Entertainment | Recognizable situation, development, earned payoff |

Keep spoken words, footage, and overlay consistent. A visual can exaggerate
just as much as a headline: staged dashboards, invented comments, and fake
before/after results are not proof. Label illustrative scenes where viewers
would otherwise mistake them for an observed event.

## Write beats that can be filmed

When production detail is requested, use a small table:
**approximate time | spoken words | visual/overlay**. Put actual spoken copy
in the script, not instructions such as "say something compelling."

Show the interesting object or result early. Add only the context needed to
understand the next step. Each beat should answer something, advance an
action, or create a justified next question. Build retention through
progress and useful detail rather than repeated promises to reveal a secret.

Every open question needs a payoff within the agreed scope. If the footage
ends with an unresolved test, say what remains unknown. Do not write an
unfilmed success into the closing line. A brief ending without a CTA is fine;
add one only when it serves the viewer and the destination permits it.

## Production checks

- Read the script aloud against the requested duration. Roughly 2-3 spoken
  words per second is an initial estimate, not a substitute for timing;
  allow pauses, demonstrations, and difficult terms. Label untested timings.
- Make the core meaning understandable with captions and legible visuals.
  Do not cover the subject with overlay or rely only on sound or color.
- Distinguish existing footage from a proposed shot. Avoid production
  requests the supplied footage cannot support.
- Check whether cropping, captions, pacing, and links fit the destination.
  Rework the opening and context when repurposing; a crop alone is not an
  adaptation.
- Use retention drop-offs as questions about clarity, relevance, pacing,
  and payoff. Do not diagnose the hook from completion rate alone.
